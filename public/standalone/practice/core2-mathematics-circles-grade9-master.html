Perpendicular Distance from Centre to Chord

Subtopic: Perpendicular from Centre to Chord
In a circle of radius $13\text{ cm}$, a chord $AB$ has length $24\text{ cm}$. Find the perpendicular distance of the chord from the centre $O$ of the circle.
Vector Figure: FIG-IMO-G09-CIRCLES-Q001.svg
Commit Initial Reasoning & Working (Prior to Assistance)
Tempting Mistake & Why It Fails: Forgetting that the perpendicular bisects the chord, attempting to use the full chord length 24 cm in Pythagoras.
Subtracted $13 - 12 = 1$ or arithmetic error.
Correct distance derived from the fundamental Pythagorean circle bisector theorem.
Confused distance with diameter quotient $24 / 4 = 6$.
Mistook $(13 - 6) = 7$ as the perpendicular leg.
Hint Ladder (3 Progressive Steps) Nudge · Tactical · Crux
Tier 1 · Conceptual Nudge

Drop a perpendicular $OM$ from centre $O$ to chord $AB$. What does $OM$ do to segment $AB$?

Tier 2 · Tactical Step

The perpendicular bisects $AB$, so $AM = MB = \frac{24}{2} = 12\text{ cm}$. In $\triangle OMA$, hypotenuse $OA = 13\text{ cm}$.

Tier 3 · Crux Move

The perpendicular from the circle centre to a chord bisects the chord, forming a right triangle with the radius.

Model Derivation & Proof Correct Choice: B ($5\text{ cm}$)
Formal Verification Verified Answer: Option B ($5\text{ cm}$)

Step-by-Step Derivation:

1. Let $OM \perp AB$ where $M$ is the foot of the perpendicular on $AB$. 2. The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord: $AM = \frac{1}{2} AB = \frac{24}{2} = 12\text{ cm}$. 3. Connect $O$ to $A$ to form right-angled $\triangle OMA$ with hypotenuse $OA = r = 13\text{ cm}$. 4. By the Pythagorean theorem: $OM^2 + AM^2 = OA^2 \implies OM^2 + 12^2 = 13^2$. 5. $OM^2 = 169 - 144 = 25 \implies OM = 5\text{ cm}$.
Verification Check:
Check chord length from distance: $AB = 2\sqrt{r^2 - d^2} = 2\sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = 2\sqrt{169 - 25} = 2\sqrt{144} = 2 \times 12 = 24\text{ cm}$. Matches stem exactly.

Tier 2 · Tactical Step

All three sides equal $r$, so the central angle is $60^\circ$. The angle subtended at the circumference is half the central angle.

Tier 3 · Crux Move

A chord equal in length to the radius forms an equilateral triangle with the center, subtending 60 degrees at the center and 30 degrees at the circumference.

Model Derivation & Proof Correct Choice: A ($60^\circ$ and $30^\circ$)
Formal Verification Verified Answer: Option A ($60^\circ$ and $30^\circ$)

By the Inscribed Angle Theorem, the angle subtended by an arc at the circumference is half the angle subtended at the centre. 5. The inscribed angle on the minor arc would be $180^\circ - 30^\circ = 150^\circ$. Reflex $\angle AOB = 360^\circ - 60^\circ = 300^\circ$, and $\frac{1}{2}(300^\circ) = 150^\circ$, confirming consistency.

Cyclic Quadrilateral Opposite Angle Bisectors Invariant

Subtopic: Cyclic Quadrilateral Angle Bisectors
Let $ABCD$ be a cyclic quadrilateral. The internal angle bisectors of opposite angles $\angle A$ and $\angle C$ intersect the circumcircle at points $P$ and $Q$ respectively. What can be concluded about the line segment $PQ$?
Commit Initial Reasoning & Working (Prior to Assistance)
Tempting Mistake & Why It Fails: Guessing parallelism to diagonals rather than using the $90^\circ$ diameter invariant.
Assumed chord length equals radius $R$ instead of $2R$.
Correct: $PQ$ is a diameter because the inscribed angle subtending it is $90^\circ$.
Parallelism to $BD$ only holds in special symmetric configurations.
Perpendicularity to $AC$ does not hold in asymmetric cyclic quads.
Hint Ladder (3 Progressive Steps) Nudge · Tactical · Crux
Tier 1 · Conceptual Nudge

Express the measure of arc $PQ$ in terms of the angle bisectors of $\angle A$ and $\angle C$.

Tier 2 · Tactical Step

$\angle A + \angle C = 180^\circ \implies \frac{1}{2}\angle A + \frac{1}{2}\angle C = 90^\circ$. An inscribed angle of $90^\circ$ subtends a diameter.

Tier 3 · Crux Move

Since opposite angles of a cyclic quad sum to 180 degrees, the sum of their half-angles is 90 degrees, forcing the line joining the arc midpoints to be a diameter.

Model Derivation & Proof Correct Choice: B ($PQ$ is a diameter of the circle)
Formal Verification Verified Answer: Option B ($PQ$ is a diameter of the circle)

Step-by-Step Derivation:

1. In cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$, opposite angles are supplementary: $\angle A + \angle C = 180^\circ$. 2. Dividing by 2: $\frac{1}{2}\angle A + \frac{1}{2}\angle C = 90^\circ$. 3. The bisector of $\angle A$ meets the circle at $P$, so arc $BP = \text{arc } PD$, meaning $\angle BAP = \angle DAP = \frac{1}{2}\angle A$. 4. The bisector of $\angle C$ meets the circle at $Q$, so arc $DQ = \text{arc } QC$, meaning $\angle DCQ = \angle BCQ = \frac{1}{2}\angle C$. 5. Connecting $P$ and $Q$ through a point on the circumference yields an inscribed angle subtended by chord $PQ$ equal to $\frac{1}{2}\angle A + \frac{1}{2}\angle C = 90^\circ$. 6. By Thales theorem, an angle inscribed in a semicircle is $90^\circ$, which implies chord $PQ$ subtends a semicircle and is strictly a diameter of the circle.
Verification Check:
Let $ABCD$ be a square (a cyclic quad with all angles $90^\circ$). Angle bisectors pass through the centre $O$, and $P, Q$ are diametrically opposite vertices. Thus $PQ$ is a diameter.

Distance between centres $O_1$ and $O_2$ is equal to radius $R = 5\text{ cm}$. The common chord $AB$ perpendicularly bisects segment $O_1 O_2$.

Tier 2 · Tactical Step

Formal Verification Verified Answer: Option A (Under inversion, transformed distances satisfy $B'D' \le B'C' + C'D'$; equality holds if and only if $C'$ lies on segment $B'D'$, meaning $A, B, C, D$ lie on a circle (or line) in cyclic order.)

Step-by-Step Derivation:

1. Perform a circular inversion centered at vertex $A$ with arbitrary radius $k$. 2. Points $B, C, D$ are mapped to $B', C', D'$ such that $AB \cdot AB' = k^2$, $AC \cdot AC' = k^2$, $AD \cdot AD' = k^2$. 3. The distance between any two inverse points $X'$ and $Y'$ is given by: $$X'Y' = \frac{k^2 \cdot XY}{AX \cdot AY}$$ 4. Apply this formula to segments among $B', C', D'$: $$B'D' = \frac{k^2 \cdot BD}{AB \cdot AD}, \quad B'C' = \frac{k^2 \cdot BC}{AB \cdot AC}, \quad C'D' = \frac{k^2 \cdot CD}{AC \cdot AD}$$ 5. In the Euclidean plane, the Triangle Inequality for points $B', C', D'$ states: $$B'D' \le B'C' + C'D'$$ 6. Substitute the inverse distance expressions: $$\frac{k^2 \cdot BD}{AB \cdot AD} \le \frac{k^2 \cdot BC}{AB \cdot AC} + \frac{k^2 \cdot CD}{AC \cdot AD}$$ 7. Divide by $k^2$ and multiply by the common denominator $AB \cdot AC \cdot AD > 0$: $$AC \cdot BD \le BC \cdot AD + AB \cdot CD$$ 8. Triangle inequality achieves equality $B'D' = B'C' + C'D'$ if and only if point $C'$ lies strictly on the straight line segment $B'D'$. 9. Under circular inversion, a straight line not passing through the inversion centre $A$ inverts to a circle passing through $A$. 10. Since $B', C', D'$ are collinear in that order on a line not containing $A$, their pre-images $A, B, C, D$ must lie on a common circle in cyclic order. 11. Thus, Ptolemy's equality $AC \cdot BD = AB \cdot CD + BC \cdot DA$ holds if and only if $ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral.
Verification Check:
Let points be complex numbers $a, b, c, d$. The cross ratio identity $(b-d)(a-c) = (b-c)(a-d) + (c-d)(a-b)$ is an algebraic identity. Taking absolute values and applying the triangle inequality $|u+v| \le |u| + |v|$ gives Ptolemy's inequality. Equality holds iff $u$ and $v$ have the same argument, which geometrically corresponds to the cross ratio being real and negative, which is the exact condition for concyclicity in order. Verified rigorously.

Conventions and Hypotheses · Circle Fundamentals & Chord Bisection

Domain: Euclidean Plane Geometry in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Let a circle $\mathcal{C}$ have centre $O$ and radius $r$. Let $AB$ be a chord of length $L$.

The Inferential Leap: A perpendicular line dropped from the centre to any chord bisects the chord, decomposing the chord geometry into two congruent right triangles with hypotenuse equal to the radius.

Step 1: Radii $OA$ and $OB$ satisfy $OA = OB = r$, establishing that $\triangle OAB$ is isosceles with base $AB$. Why valid: All points on a circle circumference are equidistant from the centre.

Step 2: Drop perpendicular $OM \perp AB$ where $M$ lies on $AB$. In right triangles $\triangle OMA$ and $\triangle OMB$, hypotenuse $OA = OB$ and leg $OM$ is shared. By RHS congruence, $\triangle OMA \cong \triangle OMB$, forcing $AM = MB = L/2$. Why valid: RHS congruence guarantees identical corresponding sides.

Step 3: In right $\triangle OMA$, by Pythagoras: $OM^2 + AM^2 = OA^2 \implies d^2 + (L/2)^2 = r^2$. Why valid: The Pythagorean theorem holds in any Euclidean right-angled triangle.

Representation Bridge · Metric to Geometric Translation

The right triangle $\triangle OMA$ maps: Leg $1 = d$ (perpendicular distance), Leg $2 = L/2$ (half-chord), Hypotenuse $= r$ (radius). Any two parameters uniquely determine the third via $d = \sqrt{r^2 - (L/2)^2}$.

Trap Clinic · Common Pitfall: Plausible wrong path is substituting full chord length $L = 24\text{ cm}$ directly into $d = \sqrt{r^2 - L^2}$, yielding negative square roots or arithmetic breakdown. Refutation and replacement rule: Always halve the chord length to isolate the right triangle leg $AM = L/2$ before invoking the Pythagorean theorem.
Independent Exit Task: If a circle has radius $r = 17\text{ cm}$ and a chord has length $L = 30\text{ cm}$, verify that the perpendicular distance is $d = \sqrt{17^2 - 15^2} = \sqrt{289 - 225} = 8\text{ cm}$.

Conventions and Hypotheses · Cyclic Quadrilaterals & Intersecting Chords

Domain: Concyclic vertex configurations. Quadrilateral $ABCD$ has all four vertices on a circle $\mathcal{C}$.

The Inferential Leap: Opposite angles of any cyclic quadrilateral sum to $180^\circ$ because their subtended central angles partition the entire $360^\circ$ perimeter of the circle.

Step 1: Inscribed angles $\angle BCD$ and $\angle BAD$ stand on complementary arcs whose central angles $\alpha + \beta = 360^\circ$. Why valid: Every point on the circle circumference belongs to either arc $BCD$ or arc $BAD$.

Step 2: By the Inscribed Angle Theorem, $\angle BAD = \beta / 2$ and $\angle BCD = \alpha / 2$. Summing them yields $\angle BAD + \angle BCD = (\alpha + \beta) / 2 = 360^\circ / 2 = 180^\circ$. Why valid: Sum of halves of two angles equals half of their total sum.

Step 3: Exterior angle to cyclic quad: if side $AB$ is produced to $E$, $\angle CBE + \angle ABC = 180^\circ$ (linear pair). Since $\angle ADC + \angle ABC = 180^\circ$, we have $\angle CBE = \angle ADC$. Why valid: Two angles supplementary to the exact same angle are equal.

Representation Bridge · Intersecting Chords Power

When chords $AB$ and $CD$ intersect at interior point $P$, $\triangle PAC \sim \triangle PDB$ by AA similarity (angles in same segment are equal). Therefore $PA / PD = PC / PB \implies PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD$.

Trap Clinic · Common Pitfall: Confusing cyclic quadrilaterals with parallelograms and assuming opposite angles are equal rather than supplementary. Refutation and replacement rule: In parallelograms opposite angles are equal ($\angle A = \angle C$); in cyclic quadrilaterals opposite angles are strictly supplementary ($\angle A + \angle C = 180^\circ$).
Independent Exit Task: If cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$ has $\angle A = 75^\circ$ and side $AB$ is produced to $E$, prove that $\angle C = 105^\circ$ and exterior $\angle CBE = 105^\circ$.

Conventions and Hypotheses · Annulus Area Invariance & Power of a Point

Domain: Concentric circles $\mathcal{C}_1$ (radius $R$) and $\mathcal{C}_2$ (radius $r$) with $R > r$. Tangent chord $AB$ of outer circle touches inner circle at $T$.

The Inferential Leap: The area of the ring between concentric circles depends exclusively on the length of the tangent chord, invariant of the individual radii $R$ and $r$.

Step 1: Radius $OT$ is perpendicular to tangent chord $AB$ at contact point $T$, bisecting the chord so that $AT = TB = L/2$. Why valid: Radius to tangent point is perpendicular to tangent line.

Step 2: In right $\triangle OTA$, hypotenuse $OA = R$ and leg $OT = r$, giving $R^2 - r^2 = AT^2 = (L/2)^2$. Why valid: Pythagorean theorem holds in right $\triangle OTA$.

Step 3: Annulus area is $\text{Area} = \pi R^2 - \pi r^2 = \pi(R^2 - r^2) = \pi(L/2)^2 = \frac{\pi L^2}{4}$. Why valid: Algebraic substitution of difference of squares into the circular area difference formula.

Representation Bridge · Invariant Annulus Geometry

Even if inner radius $r \to 0$, the annulus collapses into a single circle of diameter $L$, whose area is $\pi(L/2)^2$, exactly matching the formula.

Trap Clinic · Common Pitfall: Believing that individual values of $R$ and $r$ are required to evaluate the ring area. Refutation and replacement rule: Only the difference of squares $R^2 - r^2$ is needed, which is directly supplied by $(L/2)^2$.
Independent Exit Task: If a chord of the outer concentric circle has length $20\text{ cm}$ and is tangent to the inner circle, prove the annulus area is $\pi(10)^2 = 100\pi\text{ cm}^2$.

Conventions and Hypotheses · Dual-Circle Radical Axis & Ptolemy's Theorem

Domain: Two intersecting circles $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $\mathcal{C}_2$ intersecting at points $A$ and $B$. Secants through $A$ and $B$ intercept parallel chords.

The Inferential Leap: Chaining cyclic quadrilateral opposite-angle supplementarity across the shared radical chord transfers equal consecutive interior angles, proving lines are strictly parallel.

Step 1: In circle $\mathcal{C}_1$, quadrilateral $ABFD$ is cyclic, so $\angle D + \angle ABF = 180^\circ$. Why valid: Opposite angles of cyclic quadrilateral in $\mathcal{C}_1$ are supplementary.

Step 2: In circle $\mathcal{C}_2$, quadrilateral $ABEC$ is cyclic. Along straight line transversal $FBE$, $\angle ABE + \angle ABF = 180^\circ$ (linear pair). Since $\angle ABE + \angle C = 180^\circ$, we have $\angle C = \angle ABF$. Why valid: Exterior angle of cyclic quadrilateral equals interior opposite angle.

Step 3: Substituting yields $\angle D + \angle C = 180^\circ$. For lines $CD$ and $EF$ intersected by transversal $AD$, consecutive interior angles are supplementary, so $CD \parallel EF$. Why valid: Euclid's parallel postulate converse: supplementary consecutive interior angles guarantee parallel lines.

Representation Bridge · Ptolemy's Diagonal Product

For any cyclic quadrilateral with side lengths $a, b, c, d$ and diagonals $d_1, d_2$, Ptolemy's identity guarantees $d_1 \cdot d_2 = ac + bd$.

Trap Clinic · Common Pitfall: Multiplying adjacent sides ($ab + cd$) instead of opposite side pairs in Ptolemy's theorem. Refutation and replacement rule: Ptolemy's theorem strictly requires products of OPPOSITE pairs: $d_1 d_2 = ac + bd$.
Independent Exit Task: In a cyclic quadrilateral with consecutive sides $3, 4, 5, 6$, verify that diagonal product is $d_1 d_2 = 3(5) + 4(6) = 15 + 24 = 39$.
