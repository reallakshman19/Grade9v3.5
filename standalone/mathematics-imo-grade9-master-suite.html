Core 1A · Common-Base Exponential Equations & Multiplicative Exponent Factors

Anchored to IMO D3 Index Laws (motivated by Q26 and R1 real-exponent relations).

The Inferential Leap (Technique T02):

Transform dissimilar exponential terms into one shared positive quantity $t$, recognizing that an exponent offset creates a multiplicative factor (e.g. $3^{2x+1} = 3 \cdot 9^x$) rather than an additive equality of exponents across addition, solve a linear equation in $t$, and justify the unique real exponent with an original-equation reverse check.

Model Contract & Assumptions (Technique T14)

  • Positive base exponent rule: $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$ and $a^{m+n} = a^m \cdot a^n$.
  • Equal powers of a fixed positive base other than 1 have equal real exponents: $a^u = a^v \iff u = v$.
  • For any positive base $a > 0$, $a^z > 0$ for all real $z$; hence any substituted quantity $t$ must be strictly positive ($t > 0$).
  • Linear equations preserve solution sets when adding or subtracting equal expressions on both sides.

Formal 4-Stage Construction Path

STEP 1 · DECLARE COMMON BASE

In equation $3^{2x+1} = 9^x + 162$, observe $9 = 3^2$, so $9^x = (3^2)^x = 3^{2x}$. Define positive variable $t = 3^{2x} > 0$.

Result: $9^x = t$

Why valid: Power of a power law $(3^2)^x = 3^{2x}$ holds for all real $x$.

STEP 2 · SPLIT EXPONENT OFFSET

Rewrite $3^{2x+1}$ as $3^1 \cdot 3^{2x} = 3t$. The $+1$ in the exponent creates a multiplicative factor of 3, not an added 3.

Result: $3^{2x+1} = 3t$

Why valid: Product rule $a^{m+n} = a^m \cdot a^n$.

STEP 3 · LINEAR REDUCTION

Substitute into original equation: $3t = t + 162 \implies 2t = 162 \implies t = 81$.

Result: $t = 81$ (strictly positive, $81 > 0$)

Why valid: Preserved solution set under linear subtraction.

STEP 4 · INVERSION & REVERSE CHECK

Map back to exponent: $3^{2x} = 81 = 3^4 \implies 2x = 4 \implies x = 2$.

Check: $3^5 = 243$, $9^2 + 162 = 81 + 162 = 243$

Why valid: Exponential bases $a e 1$ are injective.

⚠️ Trap Clinic (Technique T06): Equating Exponents Across Addition

The Fallacy: Looking at $3^{2x+1} = 9^x + 162$ and writing $2x+1 = x + 162$.

Refutation: Exponents can ONLY be equated when two pure powers are equal: $a^u = a^v$. The additive $+162$ cannot be absorbed into exponents. You must isolate terms using the common base quantity $t$.

Core 1A · Universal Proof: Divisibility of Three Consecutive Integers by 6

Rigorous modular construction exhausting parity and modulo-3 residue classes.

Universal Divisibility Theorems (Properly Formatted Mathematics)

✅ Parity Lemma: Consecutive integers alternate parity: $n \equiv 0 \pmod 2$ or $n \equiv 1 \pmod 2$. In any pair $\{n, n+1\}$, exactly one is even, guaranteeing $2 \mid n(n+1)$, which implies $2 \mid P(n)$.

✅ Modulo 3 Exhaustion: By the division algorithm, an arbitrary integer $n$ satisfies exactly one residue condition:

  • If $n \equiv 0 \pmod 3$, then $3 \mid n$.
  • If $n \equiv 1 \pmod 3$, then $n+2 \equiv 3 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$, so $3 \mid (n+2)$.
  • If $n \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, then $n+1 \equiv 3 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$, so $3 \mid (n+1)$.

✅ Coprimality Synthesis: If $a \mid N$ and $b \mid N$ with $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, then $ab \mid N$. Since $\gcd(2, 3) = 1$, $6 \mid P(n)$ holds rigorously for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$.

⚠️ Trap Clinic: Non-Coprime Products (Technique T06)

Fallacy: "Since $4 \mid N$ and $6 \mid N$, it must follow that $24 \mid N$."

Refutation: The product rule strictly requires coprimality: $\gcd(a, b) = 1$. Here $\gcd(4, 6) = 2 \ne 1$. For example, $N = 12$ is divisible by 4 and by 6, but $12$ is NOT divisible by 24! The least common multiple is $\operatorname{lcm}(4, 6) = 12$, not $24$.
